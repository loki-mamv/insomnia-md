What Is the Best Bedroom Temperature for Sleep?

October 19, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: No study has pinned down one perfect number. The research is clearer about what goes wrong: a hot, humid bedroom causes more waking and less deep and REM sleep. The popular advice of 60 to 67°F (about 16 to 19°C) is a common rule of thumb, not a tested range. The best home study of older adults found the most efficient sleep between 68 and 77°F (20 to 25°C), with your bedding and personal comfort deciding the rest.

Why does room temperature matter for sleep at all?

Your body temperature follows a daily rhythm. In the evening, your core temperature starts to fall, and that drop is closely tied to falling asleep. To shed heat, your body sends more blood to your hands and feet, which then release warmth into the room.

A small but well-known experiment in Nature in 1999 found that warmer hands and feet relative to the trunk predicted faster sleep onset. In other words, your body falls asleep more easily when it can dump heat.

A room that is too warm blocks this process. A 2012 review by Okamoto-Mizuno and Mizuno describes the typical result of heat or cold exposure: more wakefulness and less REM sleep and slow wave (deep) sleep. These effects track closely with how hard your body has to work to control its temperature.

Is a hot room or a cold room worse for sleep?

It depends on what you sleep in. In lab studies where people slept nearly unclothed and uncovered, cold disrupted sleep stages more than heat did. But that is not how most people sleep.

The same review notes that in real-life conditions, with blankets and sleepwear, heat is the bigger problem for sleep stages. Heat increases waking and cuts both deep sleep and REM sleep. Cold, by contrast, did not change sleep stages when people had bedding, although it did affect heart rhythm responses during sleep.

The practical takeaway: bedding protects you well against a cool room. It does much less to protect you against a hot one. That is a large part of why most advice leans toward cooler rather than warmer.

Where does the 60 to 67°F number come from?

The 60 to 67°F range is repeated widely by sleep websites and mattress companies. We could not find a primary study that tested and established those exact limits. Treat it as a reasonable starting point, not a scientific finding.

Government health advice is less specific. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute tells people to keep the bedroom "quiet, cool, and dark" and gives no number.

The strongest real-world data come from a 2023 study of older adults from the Marcus Institute for Aging Research in Boston. Researchers tracked sleep with wearable monitors and bedroom temperature with sensors over long periods in people's own homes. Sleep was most efficient and restful when nighttime room temperature was between 20 and 25°C (68 to 77°F). When the room rose from 25°C to 30°C (77 to 86°F), sleep efficiency fell by a clinically relevant 5 to 10%.

The authors also found large differences between people. That fits common experience: one partner kicks off the covers while the other reaches for a second blanket.

Does humidity make a difference?

Yes. Moist air makes it harder for sweat to evaporate, so your body loses heat more slowly.

In a 1999 sleep lab study, seven young men slept in rooms at 29°C or 35°C (about 84 or 95°F) with either 50% or 75% relative humidity. The hot, humid condition (35°C at 75% humidity) caused more waking, lower sleep efficiency, and less deep sleep than the cooler rooms. It also kept core temperature higher and reduced REM sleep compared with the same heat at lower humidity.

The study was small and used extreme conditions, but the direction is clear. On a muggy night, a fan, dehumidifier, or air conditioner can matter as much as the thermostat number.

Are older adults more sensitive to a warm bedroom?

The evidence says yes, even at temperatures many people would call only mildly warm.

  • In a 2004 study of men around age 69, a room at 32°C (about 90°F) caused more waking and less REM sleep than 26°C (about 79°F). Their core temperature also fell less overnight.
  • In a 2022 bedroom experiment with 16 people over 65, raising the room from 27°C to 30°C (about 81 to 86°F) cut total sleep by 26.3 minutes and added 27.0 minutes of time awake. Better ventilation added 10.3 minutes of deep sleep, and the benefits of cooler air and better ventilation added together.

Heat waves raise the stakes. A 2024 systematic review in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that higher outdoor and indoor temperatures are generally linked to worse and shorter sleep worldwide. The effect was stronger on the hottest days, in the warmest regions, and in vulnerable groups. The authors found limited evidence that people adapt quickly to heat.

How can you help your body cool down at night?

You do not need a perfect thermostat to sleep well. These steps work with your body's own cooling process:

  1. Start cool and adjust. Try a room in the mid to upper 60s°F and change a few degrees at a time based on how you sleep. If you are older, you may do better toward the low 70s.
  2. Use layers, not a heavy duvet. Light, breathable bedding lets you add or remove warmth in the night without getting up.
  3. Take a warm bath or shower 1 to 2 hours before bed. A 2019 meta-analysis of 17 studies found that water at 40 to 42.5°C (104 to 108.5°F) improved self-rated sleep quality and sleep efficiency. When timed 1 to 2 hours before bed, even 10 minutes shortened the time it took to fall asleep. The warmth draws blood to the skin, which speeds the later drop in core temperature.
  4. Control humidity and airflow. A fan, open window, or dehumidifier helps sweat evaporate on warm nights.
  5. Keep feet warm if a cold room bothers you. Warm feet help heat leave the body and may help you fall asleep sooner.

Temperature is one piece of good sleep habits. Our sleep hygiene guide covers the others. If hot flashes or night sweats wake you, see our article on menopause and insomnia. Early waking that comes with racing thoughts may have more to do with stress than with the room, and anxiety.md covers that side.

If you sleep poorly in every room and every season, the bedroom is probably not the main cause. Chronic insomnia usually responds best to cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).

The bottom line

A cool, dry, well-ventilated bedroom helps your core temperature fall and protects deep and REM sleep. The 60 to 67°F range is a common guide, not a proven rule. Older adults in a real-home study slept best between 68 and 77°F. Heat and humidity cause more harm than mild cold, because blankets can fix a cool room but not a hot one. Use the number as a starting point and adjust to how you actually sleep.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it bad to sleep with the heat turned off in winter?

Not usually, as long as you have enough bedding. Research suggests a cool room with blankets does not change sleep stages much. Very cold rooms can still strain the heart and may be risky for frail older adults, so keep the room comfortable rather than cold.

Should I sleep with a fan on?

A fan can help on warm or humid nights because moving air helps sweat evaporate. In one study of older adults, better bedroom ventilation added deep sleep. If noise or a dry throat bothers you, point the fan away from your face or use a lower setting.

Does a cold shower before bed help you sleep?

The research supports a warm bath or shower, not a cold one. Warm water at about 104 to 108.5 degrees F, taken 1 to 2 hours before bed, helped people fall asleep faster in a 2019 meta-analysis. The warmth pushes blood to the skin, which then helps the body cool down.

Why do I wake up hot in the middle of the night?

Common causes include a warm or humid room, heavy bedding, alcohol, and hot flashes during perimenopause or menopause. Heat tends to increase waking and reduce deep and REM sleep. If night sweats are drenching or come with fever or weight loss, see a doctor.

Do older adults need a warmer bedroom?

Older adults are more sensitive to heat during sleep, not to mild warmth in general. A 2023 home study of older adults found the best sleep between 68 and 77 degrees F, with sleep efficiency falling as rooms got hotter. Personal comfort varied a lot between people.
