Should I Get Out of Bed If I Can't Fall Asleep?

The short answer: Yes, for most adults with ongoing insomnia. Getting up when you can't sleep is the core of stimulus control, a behavioral method first described by psychologist Richard Bootzin in 1972. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests it as a stand-alone treatment, with a conditional recommendation. If getting up is not safe for you, a sit-up-in-bed version called counter-control is an option.

What is stimulus control, and why does getting up help?

Stimulus control is based on learning. Your brain links places with what usually happens there. A kitchen table cues snacking. A desk cues work. A bed should cue sleep.

With insomnia, that link wears down. Night after night, the bed becomes the place where you lie awake, worry, and check the time. In their treatment protocol chapter, Bootzin and Michael Perlis describe how the bed and bedroom can turn into cues for "the distress and frustration of trying to fall asleep." Some people feel sleepy on the couch, then feel wide awake the moment they enter the bedroom. That pattern is a sign of this conditioning.

Getting out of bed breaks the pairing. Each time you leave instead of lying there, the bed stays tied to falling asleep rather than to struggling.

What are the stimulus control rules?

The instructions have stayed almost unchanged since the 1970s. As listed in the Bootzin and Perlis protocol, they are:

  1. Lie down to sleep only when you are sleepy.
  2. Use the bed only for sleep and sex. No reading, TV, eating, or worrying in bed.
  3. If you can't fall asleep, get up and go to another room. Return to bed only to sleep.
  4. If you still can't fall asleep, repeat step 3 as often as needed through the night.
  5. Get up at the same time every morning, no matter how much you slept.
  6. Do not nap during the day.

The authors call rules 2 and 3 to 4 the "core elements." Rule 4 is what makes the method work for middle-of-the-night waking, not only trouble falling asleep. If you tend to wake in the early hours, our guide on why you wake up at 3am covers other causes worth ruling out.

The protocol also softens the nap rule for some people. For older adults, the authors say a brief scheduled nap of 30 minutes or less may be reasonable.

How long should you wait, and should you watch the clock?

You will often hear "20 minutes." That number is a common clinical shorthand, not Bootzin's original wording. A 2024 review of insomnia care in primary care summarizes the rule as getting out of bed when not asleep after 15 to 20 minutes.

The original instructions were stricter. They say that if you are in bed "more than about 10 minutes" without falling asleep and have not gotten up, you are not following the rule. Bootzin and Perlis also cite research on a "quarter-hour rule" (no more than 15 minutes in bed awake) and describe it as manageable and effective.

The exact number matters less than the principle. Don't lie in bed for long stretches hoping sleep will arrive. The protocol states plainly that staying in bed for 60 minutes or longer is not allowed, even if you don't feel frustrated.

Should you watch the clock? No. The instructions say directly: "we do not want you to watch the clock." Checking the time tends to raise the anxiety that keeps you awake.

The protocol offers two fixes:

  • Turn the clock face away from the bed so you can't see it.
  • Use a feeling, not a number. Get up at the first sign of frustration about not sleeping, or when you notice you are clearly awake.

In the same chapter, the authors say they prefer this cue-based approach to a strict time rule. Estimating is fine.

What should you do once you are out of bed?

Go to another room and do something calm and pleasant. The protocol gives specific guidance:

  • Keep the lights dim. Even room light can shift your body clock.
  • Quiet activities are fine. Reading under a reading light or watching TV from a distance is acceptable.
  • Skip the computer. Screens held close give off more light than people expect, and email or browsing tends to be arousing.
  • Don't fall asleep on the couch. The goal is to pair sleepiness with your own bed.

Return to bed when you feel you could fall asleep. For many people this means at least 15 minutes out of bed. If watching for sleepiness feels like pressure, the authors suggest picking a set block, such as 30 or 60 minutes, before you go back.

If you wake near morning, don't just start the day early. The protocol recommends going back to bed when 45 minutes or more remain before your usual wake time.

Expect some rough nights. The authors warn you may be "up and down like a yoyo" for a few days, or at worst a couple of weeks. If racing thoughts are what keep you up, anxiety.md has more on managing worry.

Does stimulus control work on its own?

Yes, though full CBT-I has stronger support. The 2021 AASM clinical practice guideline gives a strong recommendation only to multicomponent CBT-I. It gives stimulus control as a single-component therapy a conditional recommendation. Conditional means clinicians should weigh the patient's values and preferences, not apply it to everyone by default. Sleep restriction and relaxation therapy received the same conditional rating. Sleep hygiene alone received a conditional recommendation against use.

A 2023 systematic review and network meta-analysis in the Journal of Sleep Research pooled 23 randomized studies. It found stimulus control effective compared with control conditions. The authors also found that not every instruction seems essential, including some rules meant to recondition the bedroom. They noted that study quality was generally poor, so the results call for caution.

In practice, stimulus control is usually taught inside a full program. For how the pieces fit, see our overview of CBT-I as the gold standard treatment and our separate guide to sleep restriction therapy.

What if getting out of bed at night is not safe for you?

Some people should not follow the "get up" rule as written. The Bootzin and Perlis protocol says the instructions may need changes for people who:

  • Can't easily get out of bed without help because of disability.
  • Can't get up safely because of a risk of slips and falls.
  • Lack the cognitive capacity, for example because of dementia, to follow the rules.

For these cases, counter-control is the usual alternative. The 2024 primary care review notes that older adults have a high fall risk, so getting up may not be safe. With counter-control, you stay in bed but change posture and setting when awake. For example, you sit up and read or listen to something quiet, then lie back down when sleepy.

The evidence base is small but real. In a 1986 study of counter-control, 34 adults aged 35 to 78 with sleep-maintenance insomnia cut their sleep complaint by about 30% by the end of treatment. Older participants benefited as much as younger ones.

Behavioral methods still help older adults in general. A 2023 systematic review included 15 studies with 498 older adults. Stimulus control and sleep restriction each improved sleep, with larger effects when combined.

The bottom line

If you can't fall asleep, get up rather than lying there. Aim to leave bed after roughly 15 to 20 minutes, judged by feel rather than the clock. Do something quiet in dim light, and return only when sleepy. Keep a fixed wake time and skip naps. Stimulus control carries a conditional AASM recommendation on its own and works best inside CBT-I. If falls or mobility are a concern, ask a clinician about counter-control instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I read in bed if it helps me feel sleepy?

Standard stimulus control rules say no. The bed is kept for sleep and sex only, so reading happens in another room. Some research suggests not every rule is essential, so a sleep clinician may adjust this for you.

What if I get sleepy on the couch after getting up?

Go back to your bed rather than dozing where you are. The goal is to pair sleepiness and sleep with your own bed. Falling asleep on the couch works against that link.

How many times a night should I get up?

As many times as needed. The original instructions tell you to repeat the get-up step whenever you can't fall asleep. The first few nights can be tiring, but the protocol authors expect this to settle within a few days to a couple of weeks.

Is it OK to check my phone while I'm out of bed?

It is better to avoid it. Screens held close to your face give off more light than most people realize, and messages or browsing tend to wake you up further. Dim light and a calm activity like reading are the better choice.

Does stimulus control help if I fall asleep easily but wake up in the night?

Yes. The rule to get up again whenever you can't sleep was included for middle-of-the-night waking. A small 1986 study also found a stay-in-bed version, counter-control, helped adults with sleep-maintenance insomnia.
