Does Napping Ruin Your Night Sleep?

September 6, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

  5. Are long naps bad for your health?

The short answer: A nap does not ruin night sleep by itself. Timing and length decide the effect. A nap of about 10 to 20 minutes in the early afternoon costs you almost nothing at night. A nap of an hour or more, or any nap after about 3 p.m., spends the sleep pressure you need to fall asleep at bedtime. People with insomnia are the exception. Most insomnia treatment programs ask them to stop napping, at least during treatment.

Why does a nap make it harder to fall asleep?

Two systems control when you feel sleepy. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) describes them on its body clock page. The first is sleep drive. It is a pressure that builds with every hour you stay awake. NHLBI says "a compound called adenosine seems to be one factor linked to this drive for sleep." Adenosine rises in the brain while you are awake and falls while you sleep.

The second system is your circadian clock. It follows light and dark and sets the times of day when you feel alert or sleepy. The two systems work together. The CDC's National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) puts it this way in its sleep training for nurses: pressure for sleep "gets stronger the longer we stay awake and decreases during sleep, reaching a low after a full night of good-quality sleep."

A nap spends sleep pressure. A short nap spends a little. A long nap that reaches deep sleep spends a lot. Nap late in the day and you reach bedtime with less pressure than usual. Naps do not damage sleep. They move it.

How long should a nap be?

The best controlled study on nap length is Brooks and Lack, published in Sleep in 2006 (abstract). Twenty-four healthy young adults slept about 5 hours at night. The next day they napped at 3 p.m. for exactly 5, 10, 20, or 30 minutes of sleep, or not at all. Testing ran for 3 hours.

  • The 5-minute nap did little compared with no nap.
  • The 10-minute nap gave immediate gains in sleepiness, fatigue, vigor, and cognitive performance. Some gains lasted up to 155 minutes.
  • The 20-minute nap helped, but the benefit did not appear until about 35 minutes after waking.
  • The 30-minute nap caused a period of worse alertness and performance right after waking. The benefit came later.

That post-nap slump is sleep inertia. NIOSH defines it as "a temporary disorientation and decline in performance and/or mood after awakening from sleep" (source). It usually fades within 30 minutes but lasts longer when you are sleep deprived. The cause is waking from deep sleep, which you reach after about an hour.

NIOSH's nap duration page gives a clear rule for a normal daytime schedule. A brief nap of under 20 minutes "can increase alertness for a couple of hours after the nap, with less grogginess, and does not disrupt subsequent sleep at night." If you need a longer nap, about 90 minutes is one full sleep cycle, so you wake from lighter sleep.

When is the best time to nap?

Your circadian clock has two dips in alertness. NIOSH's nap timing page says the strongest drive for sleep is between 2 and 5 a.m. A weaker dip comes "in the afternoon around 2 to 4 p.m." That dip is why a post-lunch nap feels natural. It happens whether or not you ate lunch.

The same page names the times to avoid. It is harder to fall asleep "around 11 a.m. to noon and in the evening 1 to 3 hours before your usual bedtime." A nap in that evening window is the one most likely to hurt. It drains sleep pressure right when you need it most.

A practical schedule: nap between about 1 and 3 p.m., keep it under 20 minutes, and set an alarm. With an 11 p.m. bedtime, a nap that ends by 3 p.m. leaves 8 hours to rebuild pressure. A nap at 6 p.m. leaves 5.

Should people with insomnia nap?

Here the advice changes. In chronic insomnia the problem is too little sleep pressure at bedtime. Naps make that worse. MedlinePlus, from the National Library of Medicine, gives the plain instruction for people with insomnia: "Do not take naps during the day or in the evening" (source).

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) says the same in its 2021 clinical practice guideline on behavioral and psychological treatments for chronic insomnia. The guideline strongly recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) and conditionally recommends stimulus control and sleep restriction therapy on their own. The fifth stimulus control instruction is "refrain from daytime napping." Sleep restriction is a method to "enhance sleep drive and consolidate sleep by limiting time in bed." A nap works against that goal. Our sleep restriction therapy guide and our overview of why CBT-I is the gold standard explain how these programs run.

One 2025 pilot study tested the rule. Faraut and colleagues compared 54 CBT-I patients who were allowed short naps with 54 matched patients who did not nap (Journal of Sleep Research). Both groups improved by a similar amount on the Insomnia Severity Index and on sleep efficiency. Most authorized naps were 30 minutes or less. The authors concluded that napping "may not have a significant impact on the effectiveness of CBT-I." This is one small study, not a guideline change. Ask your therapist before you add a nap.

Are long naps bad for your health?

Observational studies link long habitual naps with worse health. The largest pooled analysis is Yamada and colleagues, published in Sleep in 2015 (full text). It combined 11 prospective cohort studies with 151,588 participants followed for a mean of 11 years. Naps of 60 minutes or more per day were linked to a rate ratio of 1.82 (95% CI 1.22 to 2.71) for cardiovascular disease and 1.27 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.45) for all-cause mortality, compared with no napping. Naps under 60 minutes showed no significant link to either outcome.

Read that with care. The authors list the limits themselves. Naps were self-reported. People who are already sick nap more, so reverse causation is likely. Depression, frailty, and undiagnosed heart disease were not fully measured. A long daily nap may be a sign of poor health, not a cause. Loud snoring or heavy daytime sleepiness that forces long naps should raise the question of sleep apnea.

Who benefits from napping?

Shift workers benefit most. NIOSH describes a "prophylactic nap" before a night shift that is "long, usually lasting for a few hours" (source). The long nap lets the brain reach deep sleep and lowers the build-up of sleep pressure during the night. Studies cited by NIOSH found that naps of 1.5 to 3 hours before a shift improved alertness during the shift. At work, a brief nap of about 30 minutes can raise alertness during a night shift.

Older adults are a second group. Night sleep often becomes lighter and more fragmented with age. A 2022 systematic review in Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience pooled 15 studies of adults aged about 61 to 78 (full text). Naps improved sleepiness, fatigue, psychomotor speed, and learning. The review found "minimal evidence to indicate that napping is detrimental for older adults' nighttime sleep." Only a 120-minute nap slowed falling asleep at night, by about 6 minutes.

Anyone who slept badly for one night is a third group. A short afternoon nap is a reasonable repair. Keep the same wake time the next morning. If your energy crashes every afternoon even after a full night, the problem may not be sleep at all. thrive.md covers daytime energy, exercise, and nutrition.

The bottom line

Napping does not ruin night sleep. Long naps and late naps do. A 10 to 20 minute nap between 1 and 3 p.m. spends little sleep pressure and skips sleep inertia. A nap of an hour or more, or one within 3 hours of bedtime, pushes your sleep back. If you have chronic insomnia, follow your CBT-I program and skip naps unless your therapist agrees to a short one. If you work nights or you are older and sleep lightly, planned naps are a tool, not a threat.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 20 minute nap or a 90 minute nap better?

For a normal daytime schedule, under 20 minutes is the safer choice. It boosts alertness for a couple of hours with little grogginess and does not cut into night sleep. A 90 minute nap covers a full sleep cycle, so you wake from lighter sleep, but it spends far more sleep pressure. Save it for a night shift or a badly short night.

Why do I feel worse after a nap?

You woke from deep sleep. Sleep inertia is the grogginess and slow thinking that follows, and NIOSH says it usually clears within 30 minutes. Naps of 30 minutes or more reach deeper sleep, so the slump is more likely. Keep the nap under 20 minutes or extend it to about 90.

Can I nap if I only slept 5 hours last night?

Yes, and a short one works well. In the Brooks and Lack study, people limited to about 5 hours of sleep got immediate gains from a 10 minute nap at 3 p.m. Take it in the early afternoon and set an alarm. Then keep your normal bedtime so sleep pressure rebuilds by night.

Does a coffee nap work?

NIOSH training for night-shift nurses describes drinking caffeine and then taking a short nap right away. Caffeine takes time to act, so the nap ends about when the caffeine starts. This is a shift-work tool. It is a poor fit for the late afternoon, since the caffeine will still be active at bedtime.

My elderly parent naps every day. Is that a problem?

A short daily nap in an older adult is usually fine and can help mood and thinking. A 2022 review of 15 studies found little sign that naps hurt older adults' night sleep. A nap that regularly runs past an hour is worth a mention to their doctor. Long naps can be a sign of poor night sleep, sleep apnea, depression, or another illness.
