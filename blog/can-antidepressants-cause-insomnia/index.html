Can Antidepressants Cause Insomnia, and What Can You Do About It?

October 14, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: Yes. Insomnia is one of the most common side effects listed on the FDA labels for SSRIs, SNRIs, and bupropion. In placebo-controlled trials, it showed up in roughly 1 in 10 to 1 in 5 people on the drug, about double the placebo rate for some drugs. It often settles in the first weeks, and dose timing and cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) can help. Do not stop or change an antidepressant without talking to your prescriber.

Can antidepressants really cause insomnia?

Yes. Several widely used antidepressants are "activating." That means they can make some people feel more alert, restless, or wired, and sleep can suffer.

The FDA labels show this directly. The Lexapro (escitalopram) label lists insomnia first among the most commonly observed adverse reactions in adults with major depression. The Wellbutrin XL (bupropion) label also names insomnia among its most common adverse reactions. The Zoloft (sertraline) label lists insomnia as a common reaction in its OCD and social anxiety trials.

Insomnia is also a reason some people quit. In the Wellbutrin XL label, insomnia was the most common adverse reaction that led people to stop treatment: 2% on bupropion versus under 1% on placebo.

Not every antidepressant works this way. Some are sedating, and a few are prescribed off-label for sleep. Trazodone is the best-known example. Our article on whether trazodone works for sleep covers that drug in detail.

Which antidepressants list insomnia most often?

The labels report insomnia rates from placebo-controlled trials. These numbers compare the drug with a sugar pill in the same studies. Note that a meaningful share of people on placebo also reported insomnia, so the gap is what matters.

  • Fluoxetine (Prozac): In depression trials, insomnia occurred in 16% on fluoxetine versus 9% on placebo. Across all pooled indications it was 19% versus 10%, per the Prozac label.
  • Sertraline (Zoloft): In pooled trials across six conditions, insomnia occurred in 20% on sertraline versus 13% on placebo.
  • Escitalopram (Lexapro): In adult depression trials, 9% versus 4%. In a fixed-dose study, insomnia rose with dose: 4% on placebo, 7% at 10 mg, and 14% at 20 mg.
  • Venlafaxine XR (Effexor XR), an SNRI: Across indications in trials up to 12 weeks, 17.8% versus 9.5%, per the Effexor XR label.
  • Bupropion: In depression trials of the sustained-release form, insomnia occurred in 11% at 300 mg/day and 16% at 400 mg/day, versus 6% on placebo. In seasonal affective disorder trials of Wellbutrin XL, it was 20% versus 13%.

These figures come from different trials, doses, and patient groups. They are not a head-to-head ranking. They do show a consistent pattern: the risk is real, it is dose-related for some drugs, and most people on these medicines do not report it.

Does the time of day you take the dose matter?

For some drugs, the label sets a time of day. For others, it leaves the choice open. Here is what the labels say.

  • Bupropion XL: The label states the drug "should be administered in the morning." Its patient guide adds: "If you have trouble sleeping, do not take Wellbutrin XL too close to bedtime."
  • Fluoxetine: The label says to start the drug "in the morning." Higher doses go once in the morning or twice daily, "morning and noon."
  • Escitalopram: Once daily, "in the morning or evening."
  • Venlafaxine XR: Once daily with food, "either in the morning or in the evening at approximately the same time each day."

When a label allows morning or evening, a morning dose is a reasonable question to raise if sleep is a problem. Ask your prescriber or pharmacist before you move a dose. Some products have specific rules, such as swallowing extended-release tablets whole.

How do you tell drug-related insomnia from depression itself?

This is often hard to sort out. Insomnia is a core symptom of depression and anxiety, so poor sleep may have started before the medicine. Sleep may also be one of the last symptoms to improve.

A few clues point toward the medicine:

  • Sleep got worse soon after starting the drug or raising the dose.
  • You feel jittery, restless, or "revved up," not just low or worried.
  • The problem began after a dose was moved to the evening.

Keep a two-week sleep diary with bedtimes, wake times, and dose times. It gives your prescriber something concrete to work with.

Some changes need a prompt call, not a wait-and-see approach. The Wellbutrin XL label tells patients and families to watch for new anxiety, agitation, panic attacks, insomnia, irritability, or restlessness. It flags the early months of treatment and any dose change. Call your prescriber right away if those appear together, or if mood gets worse. For help with depression itself, depression.md covers treatment options and when to seek care.

What can you do about it without stopping your medication?

Start with your prescriber. Do not stop an antidepressant on your own. The Zoloft and Lexapro labels list insomnia among the symptoms that can follow stopping too fast, and they recommend a gradual dose reduction when possible.

Options to discuss include:

  1. Waiting a few weeks. Activating effects may ease as the body adjusts. Your prescriber can tell you how long is reasonable.
  2. Moving the dose earlier, where the label allows it.
  3. Adjusting the dose or switching to a different medicine, if the side effect does not settle.
  4. Treating the insomnia directly with CBT-I, which does not interact with your medication.

Daily habits help too. Cut caffeine by early afternoon, since it adds to a stimulating drug. Our guide on when to stop caffeine before bed gives the timing. Keep a steady wake time, even after a bad night.

Does CBT-I work for people taking antidepressants?

Yes, and it is the recommended first treatment for chronic insomnia. The American College of Physicians guideline recommends CBT-I as the initial treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia disorder. It rated that a strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence.

CBT-I has been tested alongside antidepressants. In a 2008 Stanford pilot trial, 30 adults with major depression and insomnia all took escitalopram. Half also got seven sessions of CBT-I; the others got a control therapy. Insomnia remitted in 50.0% of the CBT-I group versus 7.7% of the control group. Depression remitted in 61.5% versus 33.3%. The trial was small, so treat those numbers as promising, not final.

CBT-I usually combines a set sleep window, getting out of bed when you cannot sleep, and work on the worries that keep you up. Our explainer on why CBT-I is the gold standard walks through each part.

The bottom line

Antidepressants can cause insomnia. FDA labels for fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, venlafaxine XR, and bupropion all report higher insomnia rates on the drug than on placebo. The effect often fades, and it can often be managed by timing the dose as the label allows, adjusting the plan with your prescriber, and using CBT-I. Never stop an antidepressant abruptly on your own.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does insomnia from an SSRI usually last?

FDA labels report how often insomnia occurs, but they do not give a set duration. Many people find activating side effects ease over the first few weeks. If sleep is still poor after that, raise it with your prescriber rather than waiting it out.

Is bupropion more likely to keep me awake than an SSRI?

Bupropion trials reported insomnia in 11% to 16% of people in depression studies, versus 6% on placebo. SSRI labels report similar ranges, from 9% to 20% depending on the drug and study. The trials differ, so the numbers are not a direct comparison.

Can I take a sleeping pill with my antidepressant?

Some people do, but only under a prescriber's guidance. Sleep medicines can interact with antidepressants and add to drowsiness. Guidelines favor CBT-I first and medication as a short-term, shared decision.

Should I switch my antidepressant to the evening if it makes me tired?

Some labels, such as escitalopram and venlafaxine XR, allow morning or evening dosing. Bupropion XL and fluoxetine labels call for morning dosing. Ask your prescriber or pharmacist before you change the time you take any dose.

Can stopping an antidepressant cause insomnia?

Yes. The sertraline and escitalopram labels list insomnia among symptoms that can follow stopping. They recommend a gradual dose reduction rather than stopping abruptly, under a prescriber's care.
