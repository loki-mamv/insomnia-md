Do Sleeping Pills Raise Your Risk of Dementia?

October 2, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Nobody has proved that sleeping pills cause dementia. Several large studies link benzodiazepines and Z-drugs to a modestly higher dementia rate, and other large studies find no link. The clearest signal is for heavy, long-term use of anticholinergic sleep aids such as diphenhydramine. Poor sleep is itself an early sign of dementia, and that fact confuses the results.

What do studies of benzodiazepines show?

Benzodiazepines include temazepam, triazolam, and lorazepam. They have the most research, and the research does not agree.

Three results point to a link:

  • A 2018 meta-analysis in CNS Drugs pooled 15 studies. People who had ever used a benzodiazepine had higher odds of dementia (odds ratio 1.39).
  • The same analysis looked at studies that ignored the 5 years of use before diagnosis. The odds ratio fell to 1.30 and stayed statistically significant.
  • A 2014 case-control study in The BMJ compared 1,796 people with Alzheimer's disease to 7,184 controls in Quebec. Use that started at least 5 years earlier was linked to Alzheimer's disease (adjusted odds ratio 1.51). The link was stronger above 180 daily doses.

Three results point the other way:

  • A 2016 prospective study in The BMJ followed 3,434 adults aged 65 and older for a mean of 7.3 years. The lightest users had a slightly higher risk (hazard ratio 1.25). The heaviest users did not (hazard ratio 1.07). The authors said the results do not support a causal link.
  • A 2020 Danish registry study followed 235,465 patients with mood disorders. It found no association between benzodiazepines or Z-drugs and later dementia. The heaviest users had the lowest odds (odds ratio 0.83).
  • A 2025 meta-analysis of chronic use in older adults found a hazard ratio of 1.17 for dementia. That result was not statistically significant.

A real cause usually shows a dose-response pattern: more drug, more risk. The Quebec study found that pattern. The Seattle and Danish studies did not. This disagreement is the main reason the question stays open.

Are Z-drugs like zolpidem any different?

Z-drugs are zolpidem, eszopiclone, and zaleplon. They act on the same brain receptor as benzodiazepines, so researchers often study the two groups together.

A 2022 Spanish cohort study compared 83,138 users of these drugs with 84,652 nonusers older than 45. The raw result showed a 22% higher dementia risk in users. After adjustment for other health factors, the difference disappeared (hazard ratio 1.01).

The same study did find a signal in some groups. Z-drugs had a hazard ratio of 1.20, and risk was higher at the highest cumulative doses. The Danish study above included Z-drugs and found no association.

The Z-drug evidence is thinner than the benzodiazepine evidence, and it is equally mixed. No study shows that Z-drugs are safer for long-term brain health.

Could the insomnia itself explain the link?

Yes, in part. There are two problems, and no observational study can remove them fully.

Reverse causation. Dementia develops for years before a diagnosis. Poor sleep, anxiety, and low mood are often early symptoms. A person in that early stage is more likely to get a sleeping pill. The drug then looks like the cause, but the disease came first. Researchers call this protopathic bias.

A 2025 meta-analysis of 16 studies shows the size of this problem. Insomnia was linked to dementia overall (odds ratio 1.36). In studies with less than 5 years of follow-up, the odds ratio was 2.16. In studies with more than 5 years, it was 1.17. The link shrinks when early, undiagnosed dementia has time to show itself.

Confounding. People who take sleeping pills differ from people who do not. They have more anxiety, depression, pain, and chronic illness. Some of these conditions are also linked to dementia. If anxiety is part of your picture, anxiety.md covers its treatment.

Researchers try to correct for both problems. They ignore prescriptions from the last years before diagnosis, and they adjust for other illnesses. These steps make the link smaller. They do not always remove it.

What about antihistamine sleep aids and trazodone?

Antihistamines. Diphenhydramine and doxylamine are strongly anticholinergic. This group has the most consistent dementia signal. A 2015 cohort study in JAMA Internal Medicine followed 3,434 older adults. The heaviest long-term anticholinergic users had a higher dementia risk than nonusers (hazard ratio 1.54). First-generation antihistamines were one of the three most common drug classes in that study. Our article on nightly Benadryl use covers this in detail.

Trazodone. Doctors often prescribe this antidepressant for sleep. A 2019 UK study in PLOS Medicine matched 4,596 trazodone users with 22,980 users of other antidepressants. Dementia was more common with trazodone (hazard ratio 1.80). The median time to diagnosis was only 1.8 years. The authors said the result does not suggest a causal link. Doctors may choose trazodone for people who already have early dementia symptoms.

Orexin blockers. Suvorexant, lemborexant, and daridorexant are newer drugs. Their long-term effect on dementia risk is not established. A 2023 randomized trial gave 38 healthy adults aged 45 to 65 one night of suvorexant or placebo. The 20 mg dose lowered amyloid-beta in spinal fluid by about 10% to 20%. It also lowered one form of phosphorylated tau by about 10% to 15%. Both proteins are involved in Alzheimer's disease. The trial lasted 36 hours and did not measure memory or dementia. The authors said studies of long-term treatment are needed.

What do drug labels and guidelines say?

The official sources focus on harms that are proven.

  • FDA labels. The zolpidem (Ambien) label approves the drug for short-term treatment. Its boxed warning covers complex sleep behaviors such as sleep-walking and sleep-driving. It also describes memory gaps after a dose and next-day impairment. The suvorexant (Belsomra) label warns about daytime impairment and complex sleep behaviors.
  • Beers Criteria. The 2023 American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria apply to adults 65 and older. They say to avoid benzodiazepines and Z-drugs. The stated reasons are cognitive impairment, delirium, falls, fractures, and motor vehicle crashes. The panel also notes that Z-drugs give minimal improvement in sleep.
  • AASM guideline. The 2017 American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline rates every sleep drug recommendation as weak. It suggests against trazodone and diphenhydramine for chronic insomnia.

These short-term effects on memory and attention are well documented. They are a separate question from permanent dementia.

What should you do if you take a sleeping pill?

Do not stop a prescribed sleep medicine on your own. A sudden stop after long-term use can cause withdrawal and worse insomnia. Talk to your prescriber first.

These questions are useful at that visit:

  • Do I still need this medicine, and at this dose?
  • How long did you plan for me to take it?
  • Does my age or my other medicines raise my risk of falls or confusion?
  • Is a gradual reduction right for me?

If you and your prescriber decide to reduce the dose, our guide to tapering off sleeping pills explains the process. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) treats chronic insomnia without a drug.

See a clinician soon if you notice new memory loss, confusion, or a change in thinking. Those symptoms need an evaluation, whatever medicine you take.

The bottom line

The evidence shows an association, and it does not prove a cause. Pooled studies link benzodiazepines to dementia odds that are 30% to 39% higher. Several large studies with careful adjustment find no link. Early dementia causes poor sleep, and that explains part of the signal. Heavy, long-term use of anticholinergic sleep aids has the most consistent link. Falls, confusion, and next-day impairment are proven risks, mainly in adults over 65. Those risks are reason enough to review a long-term prescription with your prescriber.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Ambien cause dementia?

No study has proved that zolpidem causes dementia. A 2022 Spanish cohort study found a hazard ratio of 1.20 for Z-drugs, and a 2020 Danish registry study found no association. The FDA label warns about complex sleep behaviors, memory gaps after a dose, and next-day impairment.

Which sleep aid has the strongest link to dementia?

Strongly anticholinergic drugs, which include diphenhydramine and doxylamine, have the most consistent signal. A 2015 cohort study found a hazard ratio of 1.54 for the heaviest long-term anticholinergic users compared with nonusers. That result is an association and does not prove a cause.

Does the risk go away if I stop the medicine?

Studies have not answered this question. The memory and attention effects of a dose are short-term effects, and they are separate from dementia. Talk to your prescriber before you change or stop a sleep medicine, because a sudden stop can cause withdrawal.

Can untreated insomnia raise dementia risk too?

A 2025 meta-analysis of 16 studies linked insomnia to higher dementia odds (odds ratio 1.36). The link was much weaker in studies that followed people for more than 5 years. This pattern suggests that poor sleep is often an early symptom of dementia.

Are orexin blockers like Belsomra safer for the brain?

Nobody knows yet. A 2023 trial in 38 healthy adults found that one night of suvorexant lowered amyloid-beta and one form of tau in spinal fluid. The trial did not measure memory or dementia, and its authors said long-term studies are needed.
