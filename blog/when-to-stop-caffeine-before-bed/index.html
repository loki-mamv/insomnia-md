How Many Hours Before Bed Should You Stop Caffeine?

September 21, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: For most adults, stop caffeine at least 8 to 9 hours before bed, and longer for big doses. A 2023 meta-analysis found that a standard cup of coffee should be finished at least 8.8 hours before bedtime to avoid losing sleep. A 2025 trial found that 100 mg was fine up to 4 hours before bed, but 400 mg disrupted sleep even 12 hours out. If you have insomnia, a noon or early afternoon cutoff is a reasonable starting point.

What did the 0, 3, and 6 hour caffeine study find?

The study people quote is real. It is Drake and colleagues, published in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine in 2013. Researchers gave a fixed 400 mg dose of caffeine at bedtime, 3 hours before bed, and 6 hours before bed, and compared each with a placebo. Participants slept at home. Sleep was tracked with diaries and with a validated portable sleep monitor.

All three caffeine timings disturbed sleep compared with placebo. According to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine summary, the study enrolled 12 healthy normal sleepers. Even at 6 hours before bed, objectively measured total sleep time fell by more than one hour. The participants' own reports suggested they were unaware of that loss.

Two details matter when you read this study. First, 400 mg is a large single dose, roughly what the FDA describes as two to three 12 ounce cups of coffee. Second, the authors concluded that people should avoid substantial caffeine for a minimum of 6 hours before bed. That is a floor, not a safe line.

What do newer studies say about timing and dose?

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews pooled 24 studies. Caffeine cut total sleep time by 45 minutes and sleep efficiency by 7%. It added 9 minutes to the time needed to fall asleep and 12 minutes of wake time after sleep onset. Deep sleep dropped by 11.4 minutes. The authors calculated that coffee (107 mg per 250 mL) should be consumed at least 8.8 hours before bed. A standard serve of pre-workout supplement (217.5 mg) needed at least 13.2 hours.

The same group then ran a randomized, double-blind crossover trial published in Sleep in 2025. Twenty-three healthy young men with moderate caffeine habits took placebo, 100 mg, or 400 mg at 12, 8, and 4 hours before bed. Sleep was measured at home with partial polysomnography and diaries.

  • 100 mg: no significant effect on objective or self-rated sleep at any timing, including 4 hours before bed.
  • 400 mg: delayed sleep and changed sleep architecture when taken within 12 hours of bed, and fragmented sleep within 8 hours.
  • How it felt: perceived sleep quality dropped 34% only at the 4 hour timing, not at 8 or 12 hours.

The takeaway is that dose and timing work together. A small cup in the early afternoon may be fine for many people. A large dose may still show up in your sleep a full 12 hours later. The trial excluded women, so its numbers may not transfer directly to them.

Why does caffeine last so long, and why does it vary so much?

Caffeine is cleared by the liver, mostly by one enzyme. A 2021 systematic analysis of caffeine pharmacokinetics in Frontiers in Pharmacology notes that about 95% of caffeine clearance is due to the liver enzyme CYP1A2. Genes, smoking, hormones, pregnancy, liver disease, and some drugs all change how fast that enzyme works.

Half-life is the time it takes for half the caffeine to leave your blood. In an American Medical Association interview, a physician put the typical half-life at about four to five hours. She said it can range from an hour and a half to as much as nine hours. After one half-life, half the dose remains. After two, a quarter remains.

Several groups clear caffeine more slowly:

  • Pregnancy: a 1981 study in the European Journal of Clinical Pharmacology measured a mean half-life of 3.4 hours in men and non-pregnant women, versus 8.3 hours (range 3 to 16) in pregnant women. Values usually returned to normal within a month after delivery.
  • Oral contraceptives: in a 1985 study of women on low-dose estrogen pills, the half-life averaged 7.88 hours versus 5.37 hours in matched controls.

Smokers go the other way. In a 1982 Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics study, half-life averaged 190 minutes in smokers and 276 minutes in nonsmokers. People who quit smoking can find that their usual coffee suddenly hits harder and later.

How much caffeine is in common drinks?

The cutoff that works for you depends on how much you drink, so the amount matters as much as the clock. The FDA consumer page on caffeine gives these typical ranges for a 12 ounce drink:

  • Regular brewed coffee: 113 to 247 mg
  • Energy drink: 41 to 246 mg
  • Caffeinated soft drink: 23 to 83 mg
  • Black tea: 71 mg
  • Green tea: 37 mg

The USDA FoodData Central database lists brewed coffee at 40 mg per 100 grams, which works out to about 95 mg in an 8 ounce cup. Its entry for restaurant espresso is 212 mg per 100 grams, or about 63 mg per fluid ounce. Coffee shop sizes are often 16 or 20 ounces, so one large afternoon coffee can land near the high doses used in the studies above.

The FDA cites 400 mg a day as an amount not generally associated with negative effects in most adults. That is a daily safety figure. It says nothing about when to stop for good sleep.

Where does hidden caffeine come from?

Many people cut off coffee at noon and still take in caffeine at night. The FDA lists energy and protein bars, ice cream, chewing gum, dietary supplements, and over-the-counter medicines as products that may contain added caffeine.

  • Pain relievers: the Excedrin Migraine label lists 65 mg of caffeine per caplet, with an adult dose of 2 caplets. The label warns that this dose has about as much caffeine as a cup of coffee and can cause sleeplessness.
  • Decaf: the FDA says decaf coffee typically has 2 to 15 mg in an 8 ounce cup. That is small, but not zero.
  • Dark chocolate: USDA FoodData Central lists 70% to 85% dark chocolate at 80 mg per 100 grams, about 23 mg per ounce.
  • Pre-workout powders and evening teas: check the label, since products vary widely.

The FDA also notes that caffeine from chocolate chips or similar ingredients may not appear by name in the ingredient list.

What cutoff makes sense if you already have insomnia?

Studies of healthy sleepers set the minimum. If you already struggle to sleep, there is less margin. Several practical points follow from the evidence:

  1. Count backward from your usual bedtime. For a 10:30 pm bedtime, the 8.8 hour figure puts your last regular coffee near 1:30 pm.
  2. Stop earlier for large drinks, energy drinks, or pre-workout products, and if you are pregnant or use estrogen-containing birth control.
  3. Do not rely on how you feel. In both the 2013 and 2025 studies, people underestimated the effect on their sleep.
  4. Try a two week experiment with an earlier cutoff and a sleep diary, then compare.

The bottom line

There is no single number for everyone, but the research points in one direction. A typical cup of coffee is best finished about 9 hours before bed. Small doses may be fine closer to bedtime, while large doses can reach sleep 12 hours later. Pregnancy and oral contraceptives slow caffeine clearance, so an earlier cutoff makes sense for those groups. Count every source, including tea, soda, chocolate, and pain relievers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 2 pm too late for coffee?

It depends on your bedtime and the size of the cup. A 2023 meta-analysis suggested finishing a standard coffee about 8.8 hours before bed, so 2 pm fits a bedtime near 11 pm. A large coffee shop drink may need an earlier cutoff.

Can I be used to caffeine so that it no longer affects my sleep?

Evidence for full tolerance to caffeine's sleep effects is unclear. The 2025 trial in Sleep studied habitual caffeine users and still found sleep changes with 400 mg taken up to 12 hours before bed. People in the studies often did not notice the loss.

Does decaf coffee in the evening keep you awake?

Decaf is not caffeine free. The FDA says an 8 ounce cup typically has 2 to 15 mg of caffeine. That is well below the 100 mg dose that showed no sleep effect at 4 hours in a 2025 trial, but very sensitive people may still notice it.

Why does caffeine affect me more than my partner?

People clear caffeine at very different rates. Genes affecting the liver enzyme CYP1A2, smoking, pregnancy, and estrogen-containing birth control all change how long caffeine stays in the body. Smokers clear it faster, and pregnant women clear it much more slowly.

Is green tea a better afternoon choice than coffee?

The FDA lists green tea at about 37 mg per 12 ounces, compared with 113 to 247 mg for the same amount of brewed coffee. A smaller dose is less likely to disrupt sleep. The timing still matters, especially with several cups.
