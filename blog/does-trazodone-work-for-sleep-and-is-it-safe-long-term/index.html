Does Trazodone Work for Sleep, and Is It Safe to Take Long Term?

October 11, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Trazodone gives a small improvement in how people rate their sleep, and it cuts night-time awakenings. It does not clearly help people fall asleep faster. The FDA approves it only for depression, so insomnia use is off-label, and the main US sleep medicine guideline suggests against it. No trial has tested long-term nightly use for insomnia, so long-term safety is unproven.

Is trazodone approved for insomnia?

No. The FDA label for trazodone hydrochloride tablets lists one use: major depressive disorder in adults. The label gives 1981 as the year of first US approval. Every dose on the label is a depression dose.

A prescription for sleep is therefore off-label. The FDA has not approved trazodone as a sleep drug. A 2018 Cochrane review of antidepressants for insomnia made the same point: antidepressant use for insomnia is widespread, yet none is licensed for it. The review described the trazodone studies as low-dose and short-term.

If low mood is part of the picture, that changes the conversation with your prescriber. depression.md covers depression, the condition trazodone is approved to treat.

What do guidelines say about trazodone for sleep?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) published its guideline on drug treatment of chronic insomnia in 2017. It states: "We suggest that clinicians not use trazodone as a treatment for sleep onset or sleep maintenance insomnia (versus no treatment) in adults." The AASM graded that recommendation as weak.

Weak has a specific meaning here. The guideline says a weak recommendation reflects lower certainty and "should not be construed as an indication of ineffectiveness."

The American College of Physicians takes a broader position. Its 2016 guideline recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the initial treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia. Medication is a shared decision about short-term use when CBT-I alone has not worked. Our explainer on why CBT-I is the gold standard covers that treatment.

What did the trials find?

The trials show a modest benefit that people feel more than sleep labs measure. Four pooled analyses give the picture:

  • Cochrane, 2018. Seven trials used trazodone. Three of them (370 participants) could be pooled, and they showed a moderate improvement in subjective sleep outcomes over placebo. Two trials with sleep-lab recordings (169 participants) found little or no difference in sleep efficiency. The evidence was low quality.
  • Sleep Medicine, 2018. A meta-analysis of seven placebo-controlled trials covered 429 patients. People on trazodone rated their sleep quality better and woke fewer times. Time to fall asleep, total sleep time, and time awake in the night did not differ significantly from placebo.
  • Scientific Reports, 2022. A meta-analysis of 11 sleep-lab trials with 466 participants found about 40 more minutes of total sleep and about 19 fewer minutes to reach persistent sleep. The authors rated that evidence very low and low quality. The odds of daytime drowsiness were about 2.5 times as high with trazodone.
  • CNS Drugs, 2024. The largest review pooled 44 trials with 3,935 participants who had sleep problems of many kinds. Sleep-lab total sleep time rose by about 28 minutes. The sleep time that people reported did not change (0.73 minutes). Sleep quality improved, and time awake in the night fell by about 13 minutes.

Two points stand out. First, the sleep-onset result is inconsistent: one analysis found a gain and another found none. Second, the cost showed up in the same data. In the 2024 review, people on trazodone were 2.3 times as likely to drop out because of adverse effects.

The trials were also short. The 2022 analysis accepted trials that lasted as little as one week, and the 2024 review set a minimum of five days of treatment. For how trazodone sits next to other drug classes, see our comparison of sleep medications.

What side effects and warnings does the label list?

The label data come from depression studies, which used antidepressant doses. Rates at the lower doses used for sleep may differ, and no label reports them. In outpatient studies on the label, drowsiness affected 41% of people on trazodone and 20% on placebo. Dizziness or light-headedness affected 28% and 15%. Syncope (fainting) affected 5% and 1%.

The label carries these warnings:

  • Boxed warning. Antidepressants increased the risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors in pediatric and young adult patients in short-term studies. Trazodone is not approved for pediatric patients.
  • Next-day impairment. Trazodone may cause somnolence or sedation. The label says to be cautious about driving until you are reasonably certain the drug does not affect you.
  • Low blood pressure on standing. The label reports orthostatic hypotension and syncope.
  • Priapism. Men with an erection that lasts more than 4 hours, painful or not, should stop the drug and seek emergency care. Untreated priapism can cause irreversible damage.
  • Heart rhythm. Trazodone prolongs the QT interval. The label says to avoid it in people with known QT prolongation or a history of cardiac arrhythmias.
  • Serotonin syndrome. The risk rises with other serotonergic drugs, such as triptans, tramadol, lithium, and St. John's Wort. Use with an MAOI, or within 14 days of stopping one, is contraindicated.
  • Bleeding. Aspirin, NSAIDs, warfarin, and other anticoagulants may increase the risk.

The Cochrane review also found more adverse effects with trazodone than with placebo in insomnia trials. It named morning grogginess, dry mouth, and thirst.

Is trazodone riskier for older adults?

The evidence points to caution, but it is observational and mixed. The label says experience in elderly patients is limited and that trazodone "should be used with caution in geriatric patients." It adds that serotonergic antidepressants have been linked to low blood sodium in elderly patients.

Three studies looked at falls:

  • A Medicare claims study covered 1,699,913 beneficiaries aged 65 and older. People treated with an insomnia drug had over twice the odds of a fall compared with matched controls (odds ratio 2.34). Trazodone and benzodiazepines carried the greatest risk. The authors warned about residual confounding, because people who get these drugs may be sicker. Three authors were employees of the drug maker Eisai.
  • A 2025 study of 123 outpatients aged 75 and older with high blood pressure found a larger blood pressure drop on standing in trazodone users. Syncope and falls occurred in 58.3% of trazodone users and 21.2% of the others. Only 12 people took trazodone, and the link did not hold after adjustment for dementia.
  • A study of 31,055 hemodialysis patients compared new users of zolpidem and trazodone. Zolpidem starters had a higher 30-day risk of a fall-related fracture (hazard ratio 1.71).

So trazodone may be less risky than zolpidem in some groups, but that does not make it free of fall risk. An older adult who gets up at night should tell the prescriber about any dizziness or falls.

Is it safe long term, and how do you stop?

Nobody has shown that it is. The Cochrane authors found "relatively few, mostly small studies with short-term follow-up." They found no evidence for long-term antidepressant use for insomnia. The question is unanswered. The trials did not show long-term harm, because they did not look.

The label states that trazodone is not a controlled substance, and that clinical studies showed no sign of drug-seeking behavior. It also says abuse potential was not systematically studied.

Stopping needs a plan. The label describes a discontinuation syndrome with serotonergic antidepressants, particularly after an abrupt stop. Symptoms include nausea, sweating, irritability, dizziness, anxiety, headache, and insomnia. The label recommends a gradual dose reduction whenever possible. Your prescriber sets that schedule. Our guide to getting off long-term sleeping pills covers the taper process for other sleep drugs.

The bottom line

Trazodone can make sleep feel better and reduce awakenings, but the measured gains are modest and the trials were short. It is approved for depression, not insomnia, and the AASM suggests against it for sleep. The label lists real risks: next-day drowsiness, fainting, priapism, QT prolongation, and serotonin syndrome. If you take it now, do not stop on your own. Ask your prescriber whether it still earns its place, and whether CBT-I is an option.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is trazodone a controlled substance?

No. The FDA label states that trazodone is not a controlled substance. The label also says its abuse potential was not systematically studied, although clinical studies showed no drug-seeking behavior. Stopping abruptly can still cause discontinuation symptoms.

Can I drive the morning after I take trazodone?

Be careful until you know how it affects you. The label says trazodone may cause somnolence or sedation and may impair the ability to do hazardous tasks. It tells patients to be cautious about driving until they are reasonably certain the drug does not affect them adversely.

Can I take trazodone with another antidepressant or a migraine drug?

Ask your prescriber and pharmacist first. The label warns that serotonin syndrome is more likely when trazodone is combined with other serotonergic drugs, including triptans, tricyclic antidepressants, tramadol, lithium, and St. John's Wort. Use with an MAOI, or within 14 days of stopping one, is contraindicated.

Does trazodone change deep sleep or REM sleep?

A 2022 meta-analysis of 11 sleep-lab trials found more stage N3 (deep) sleep with trazodone and no clear effect on REM sleep. The authors rated the N3 evidence as very low quality. Treat the deep-sleep finding as uncertain.

Does trazodone help when insomnia comes with depression?

Trials in that group show a benefit. A 2026 meta-analysis of 10 randomized trials with 1,029 depressed patients found improved sleep quality scores and lower depression scores with trazodone. Somnolence, sedation, and blurred vision were more frequent, so the benefit comes with a cost.
