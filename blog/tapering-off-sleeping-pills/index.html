How Do You Get Off Long-Term Sleeping Pills Safely?

September 11, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: You come off a long-term sleeping pill with a slow, planned dose reduction that your prescriber manages. Do not stop on your own. The FDA warns that stopping a benzodiazepine abruptly, or cutting the dose too fast, can cause withdrawal reactions including seizures. Pairing the taper with cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) raised the share of long-term users who finished drug-free from 48% to 85% in one randomized trial.

Why is stopping a sleeping pill abruptly risky?

On September 23, 2020, the FDA required an updated boxed warning on every benzodiazepine. The agency states that physical dependence can occur when benzodiazepines are taken steadily for several days to weeks, even when taken exactly as prescribed. Stopping them abruptly, or reducing the dose too quickly, can produce withdrawal reactions including seizures. The FDA calls those reactions life-threatening, and its instruction is to use a gradual taper.

The Z-drugs carry a version of the same risk. The FDA label for zolpidem tartrate reports withdrawal signs and symptoms after rapid dose decrease or abrupt discontinuation. The label describes a range from mild dysphoria and insomnia up to a withdrawal syndrome with abdominal and muscle cramps, vomiting, sweating, tremors, convulsions, and delirium. That same label tells patients to talk to their provider about slowly stopping the drug.

Take this part literally. Long-term use of a benzodiazepine or a Z-drug is not something to end with a decision one evening. If you are not sure which class your pill belongs to, start with our comparison of sleep medications and then bring the answer to your prescriber.

What is rebound insomnia, and how long does it last?

Rebound insomnia is a short, sharp worsening of sleep right after a hypnotic stops. It is briefly worse than the insomnia you had before treatment, and then it fades. The 2023 Alliance for Sleep clinical practice guideline on switching or deprescribing hypnotic medications found rebound mainly with short-acting and mid-acting benzodiazepines and with zolpidem. For the Z-drugs it typically lasted only one night. For benzodiazepines it ran several days.

Rebound is not your original insomnia coming back, and it is not a full withdrawal syndrome. The difference matters, because rebound resolves by itself. Many people read two bad nights as proof they cannot sleep without the drug, and they restart. Knowing the pattern before the taper starts is one reason guidelines put therapy alongside the dose reduction.

Withdrawal can last far longer than rebound. The FDA describes a protracted withdrawal syndrome that can appear beyond four to six weeks after stopping, with symptoms lasting weeks to as long as 12 months. That outcome is not the usual course, and a gradual taper is what lowers the risk.

Which sleep medicines need a taper, and which do not?

Not every insomnia drug causes physical dependence. The Alliance for Sleep guideline sorts them:

  • Benzodiazepines should be tapered, and the guideline says CBT-I should be provided while the taper runs.
  • Zolpidem and eszopiclone should be tapered, particularly at higher doses.
  • The guideline states there is no need to taper dual orexin receptor antagonists, doxepin, or ramelteon.
  • Zaleplon can be switched directly, without a taper.

Stopping a drug that needs no taper can still be followed by the return of insomnia. That is a treatment gap, not a withdrawal reaction, and it has a different fix. Your prescriber still decides, because the dose, how long you have taken it, and other medicines all change the picture.

Does adding CBT-I improve the odds of getting off?

Yes, and the trial numbers are specific. A randomized trial in the American Journal of Psychiatry enrolled 76 older adults (mean age 62.5 years) who had used a benzodiazepine for sleep for a mean of 19.3 years. They were assigned to a 10-week supervised withdrawal program, CBT-I alone, or supervised withdrawal plus CBT-I.

All three arms cut benzodiazepine quantity by 90% and frequency by 80%. After the initial intervention, 85% of the taper-plus-CBT-I group were benzodiazepine-free, compared with 48% for the taper alone and 54% for CBT-I alone. Across the whole sample, 63% were drug-free within an average of 7 weeks. The authors reported no significant withdrawal symptoms or adverse events tied to the tapering, and the reductions held at the 12-month follow-up.

A Canadian deprescribing guideline reached the same direction with one caveat. It found that adding CBT to the tapering intervention improves cessation rates versus tapering alone, but that the added benefit is not maintained in the long term.

CBT-I is also the strongest recommendation in behavioral sleep medicine on its own. The 2021 American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline gives multicomponent CBT-I a strong recommendation for chronic insomnia in adults, and it recommends against sleep hygiene as a standalone treatment. The NHLBI calls CBT-I the first treatment option for long-term insomnia, usually run over 6 to 8 weeks. Our explainers on why CBT-I is the gold standard and on sleep restriction therapy cover what the sessions actually ask of you.

What do deprescribing guidelines recommend?

The Canadian deprescribing guideline for benzodiazepine receptor agonists makes two recommendations. It strongly recommends offering a slow taper to adults 65 and older who take one of these drugs, regardless of how long they have used it. For adults 18 to 64 who have used the drug most days for more than four weeks, it suggests tapering. It also reports that severe withdrawal events such as seizures did not appear when the drug was tapered.

Published schedules share a shape rather than a single formula. The Alliance for Sleep guideline describes reductions on the order of 10% to 25% at intervals of one to several weeks, with the pace easing near the end. Read that as the general form of published guidance, not as a plan to run by yourself. Your prescriber sets the steps, the intervals, and the points where you hold at a dose instead of dropping again.

Both guidelines make the same point about the conversation. People accept deprescribing more readily when they understand the reason, help build the plan, and get behavioral support rather than a bare instruction to stop.

How long does coming off usually take?

Plan in weeks to months, not days. In the trial above, a structured 10-week program left 63% of participants drug-free within an average of 7 weeks, after nearly two decades of nightly use. Guidelines allow slower reductions near the finish, so some people take longer, and a pause at one dose is a normal part of the process rather than a failure.

Expect anxiety to be part of it. Short-lived rebound anxiety can follow discontinuation of a benzodiazepine, and anxiety is common in people who have used hypnotics for years. If anxious arousal is the thing keeping you awake, the resources at anxiety.md are worth reading alongside your taper plan. A 2026 pilot trial also tested pharmacist-led deprescribing delivered in parallel with digital CBT-I, which suggests the support does not have to come from a sleep specialist.

The bottom line

Getting off a long-term sleeping pill is a medical project with a good success rate, and it is not a willpower test. Ask your prescriber for a taper plan, and ask for CBT-I at the same time rather than after. The 85% versus 48% gap in the randomized trial is the case for doing both together. Never taper a benzodiazepine or a Z-drug on your own, and never stop one abruptly because a refill ran out.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I cut my own dose without telling my doctor?

No. The FDA requires a boxed warning on all benzodiazepines stating that reducing the dose too quickly can cause withdrawal reactions, including seizures. The person who wrote the prescription needs to set the steps and monitor you. Self-directed cuts are the main avoidable hazard here.

Will my sleep be permanently worse once the pills are gone?

The trial evidence says no. In the American Journal of Psychiatry trial, benzodiazepine reductions held at 12 months and sleep improvements became more noticeable over that period. The authors noted that the gains may take several months of abstinence to show up.

Does switching to a different sleep drug count as getting off?

Not really, though switching is a recognized step. The Alliance for Sleep guideline handles switching and deprescribing together, and it advises tapering zolpidem or eszopiclone and then waiting one to two days before starting something new. Talk with your prescriber about whether the goal is a different drug or no drug.

What should I do if one taper step feels unbearable?

Call your prescriber before changing anything. Published guidelines expect the pace to slow near the end and allow holding at a dose instead of dropping again. Going back to an old dose on your own, or doubling up on a bad night, undoes the plan.

Do I need a sleep specialist to do this?

Not necessarily. A 2026 pilot trial paired clinical pharmacist-led deprescribing with asynchronous CBT-I and reported meaningful reductions in sleep medication use. Your prescriber, a pharmacist, and a digital CBT-I program can cover the work in many cases.
