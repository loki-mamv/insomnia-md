Do Sleep Trackers Actually Measure Sleep Accurately?

September 4, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents
  1. How do sleep trackers estimate sleep?
  2. How well do trackers tell sleep from wake?
  3. Can a tracker measure deep sleep and REM?
  4. Can a sleep tracker make insomnia worse?
  5. When is a sleep tracker useful?
  6. When should I get a real sleep study?
  7. The bottom line

The short answer: Partly. Consumer trackers like Fitbit, Apple Watch, Oura, and Whoop are good at telling when you are asleep, but poor at noticing when you are awake in bed. Their sleep-stage readouts (light, deep, REM) agree only moderately with a lab sleep study. A tracker can show trends over weeks. It cannot diagnose insomnia, sleep apnea, or any other sleep disorder.

How do sleep trackers estimate sleep?

A tracker never sees your brain. The medical test for sleep, polysomnography, records "brain waves" and monitors "heart rate, breathing, and the oxygen level in your blood during a full night of sleep" (NHLBI, Sleep Studies). Sensors go on the scalp, face, chest, limbs, and a finger. A trained technologist then scores each 30-second block of the night as wake, light sleep, deep sleep, or REM.

A wrist or finger device has two main inputs: an accelerometer that senses movement and an optical sensor that estimates heart rate. Baron and colleagues put it plainly in 2017: these devices "measure movement and not electroencephalograms" (Baron et al., 2017). Newer models add heart rate variability and skin temperature. A company algorithm turns those signals into a guess about your sleep stage. The algorithms are private, so outside researchers cannot check how they work.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) states that "future validation, access to raw data and algorithms, and FDA oversight are needed" (AASM Position Statement on Consumer Sleep Technology). Until then, treat each number on the app as an estimate.

How well do trackers tell sleep from wake?

This is where trackers do best, with one important gap. Chinoy and colleagues tested seven consumer devices against polysomnography in 34 healthy young adults over three lab nights, including one night with disrupted sleep (Chinoy et al., 2021, Sleep). The wearables were the Fitbit Alta HR, Garmin Fenix 5S, Garmin Vivosmart 3, and Fatigue Science Readiband.

Two numbers tell the story:

  • Sensitivity (catching sleep) was 0.93 to 0.99. When you were asleep, the device almost always said so.
  • Specificity (catching wake) was 0.18 to 0.54. When you were awake in bed, the device often called it sleep.

Five of the seven devices beat the research actigraph, which scored 0.39 on specificity. So consumer devices are not worse than the research standard for sleep-wake. They share the same blind spot: lying still while awake looks like sleep.

A 2025 meta-analysis pooled 24 studies and 798 participants who wore wrist devices from Fitbit, Garmin, Whoop, Apple, Xiaomi, and others (Lee et al., 2025, JCSM). Compared with polysomnography, the devices on average:

  • Underestimated total sleep time by 16.854 minutes.
  • Underestimated sleep efficiency by 4.691 percentage points.
  • Overestimated time to fall asleep by 2.574 minutes.
  • Overestimated wake after sleep onset by 13.255 minutes.

The authors concluded the devices "are not as reliable as polysomnography in measuring key sleep parameters." A 15-minute error on a single night is small. It matters more when you compare one night against another and read meaning into the gap.

Can a tracker measure deep sleep and REM?

Not well. Chinoy's team found "mixed and often poor results" for sleep staging, with "high variability" from night to night. A 2025 study in Sleep Advances tested six current wrist devices in 62 adults (Schyvens et al., 2025). It reported agreement with the lab using Cohen's kappa, where 1.0 is perfect and 0 is chance:

  • Apple Watch Series 8: 0.53 (moderate)
  • Fitbit Sense: 0.42 (moderate)
  • Fitbit Charge 5: 0.41 (moderate)
  • Whoop 4.0: 0.37 (fair)
  • Withings Scanwatch: 0.22 (fair)
  • Garmin Vivosmart 4: 0.21 (fair)

Sensitivity for sleep was above 90 percent for every device. Specificity for wake ranged from 29.39 to 52.15 percent, the same weakness Chinoy found four years earlier. The authors said the higher-scoring devices "could be effectively used to track prolonged and significant changes in sleep architecture." That is a modest claim. It means a big shift over many nights may be real. A single "low deep sleep" score is not a finding.

Can a sleep tracker make insomnia worse?

Yes, for some people. In 2017 Baron and colleagues described three patients who came to a sleep clinic because their trackers said they slept badly. They named the pattern "orthosomnia," after orthorexia, the unhealthy preoccupation with eating perfectly. Patients showed "a perfectionistic quest for the ideal sleep in order to optimize daytime function."

The striking finding was trust. One patient had a normal lab sleep study and still asked, "Then why does my Fitbit say I am sleeping poorly?" The authors wrote that tracker data "often feels more consistent with their experience of sleep than validated techniques, such as polysomnography or actigraphy." For a person with insomnia, a red score at 7 a.m. adds worry to a day that is already hard. Worry about sleep is one of the engines of chronic insomnia. If your nights are tangled up with anxious thoughts, our article on anxiety and insomnia and the resources at anxiety.md cover that loop.

A practical rule: if checking your score changes your mood or your plans for the day, stop checking it for two weeks. Many people sleep better when they stop measuring.

When is a sleep tracker useful?

A tracker earns its place in three situations.

  1. Bedtime and wake time. The device logs when you got in bed and when you got up with little effort. That schedule record is the raw material for sleep restriction therapy, a core part of CBT-I. Baron's group said that folding tracker use into CBT-I "will be important."
  2. Trends over weeks. If total sleep time drops by an hour across a month of late shifts, the device will show it. The 17-minute average bias does not change a trend of that size.
  3. A conversation starter with a clinician. The AASM says consumer sleep technology "may be utilized to enhance the patient-clinician interaction when presented in the context of an appropriate clinical evaluation."

A tracker is not useful for grading a single night, chasing a deep-sleep target, or deciding whether you have a sleep disorder. The AASM is direct on that point: "Given the lack of validation and United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) clearance, CSTs cannot be utilized for the diagnosis and/or treatment of sleep disorders at this time."

When should I get a real sleep study?

Some symptoms call for the real test, whatever the app says. MedlinePlus lists "loud snoring during sleep, waking up from sleep and gasping for breath, trouble falling and/or staying asleep, or daytime sleepiness" as reasons to get a sleep study (MedlinePlus, Sleep Study). The AASM estimates nearly 30 million U.S. adults have obstructive sleep apnea, and about 23.5 million of them do not know it (AASM, Recognizing Sleep Apnea Warning Signs).

The AASM warning signs are snoring, choking or gasping during sleep, daytime sleepiness, obesity, and high blood pressure. Morning headaches and waking often to urinate are added clues. A tracker cannot detect breathing pauses. Some devices flag low blood oxygen or "breathing disturbances," but those features are screening hints, not a diagnosis. Read our guide to sleep apnea signs if any of this sounds familiar.

Two test options exist. An in-lab polysomnogram records the full set of signals overnight in a private room. A home sleep apnea test uses "fewer sensors" and, per MedlinePlus, is "used to help diagnose sleep apnea" but cannot diagnose other sleep disorders. Your clinician picks the right one. Insomnia itself is usually diagnosed by interview and a sleep diary, not by a lab night.

The bottom line

Sleep trackers measure movement and heart rate, then guess. They catch sleep well (93 to 99 percent sensitivity in Chinoy 2021), miss much of your awake time in bed, and stage sleep with only fair to moderate agreement against a lab. Use one for schedule logging and long trends. Ignore single-night scores. If the score is making you anxious, take the device off. If you snore, gasp, or fall asleep during the day, skip the app and ask for a sleep study.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Oura ring more accurate than a wrist tracker?

Finger and wrist devices use the same kinds of sensors: movement and optical heart rate. Independent lab comparisons show all consumer devices detect sleep well and detect wake poorly. No consumer device has been shown to match polysomnography for sleep stages.

Why does my tracker say I got less sleep than I think I did?

In a 2025 meta-analysis of 24 studies, wrist devices underestimated total sleep time by about 17 minutes on average and overestimated wake after sleep onset by about 13 minutes. Night-to-night error can be larger. A small gap is normal and does not mean you have a sleep problem.

Should I stop wearing my tracker if I have insomnia?

If the score raises your worry or changes how you plan the day, take a two-week break. Clinicians call tracker-driven sleep anxiety orthosomnia. A simple paper sleep diary gives a CBT-I therapist what they need without a nightly grade.

Can a smartwatch detect sleep apnea?

No consumer watch can diagnose sleep apnea. Some devices estimate blood oxygen or flag breathing disturbances, but the AASM says consumer sleep technology cannot be used to diagnose or treat sleep disorders. Loud snoring, gasping, or daytime sleepiness should lead to a sleep study.

What does a sleep study measure that a tracker cannot?

A polysomnogram records brain waves, eye movements, muscle activity, breathing, heart rate, and blood oxygen at the same time. Brain waves define sleep stages, and a tracker has no brain-wave sensor. A home sleep apnea test measures fewer signals and is only for diagnosing sleep apnea.
