Why Do I Wake Up With My Heart Pounding?

The short answer: Most of the time, a pounding heart on waking is a stress response, not heart disease. A nightmare, a burst of panic, a drop in blood sugar, a hot flash, or a pause in breathing can all set off an adrenaline surge that wakes you. MedlinePlus says palpitations are not serious most of the time. Call 911 if the pounding comes with chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, or dizziness.

What is happening when you wake with a racing heart?

Doctors call the feeling palpitations. MedlinePlus defines them as a sense that your heart is pounding or racing. You can feel them in your chest, throat, or neck. Some people feel skipped beats or a flutter instead.

A normal resting heart rate is 60 to 100 beats per minute. Above 100 is called tachycardia. The rhythm can be normal or abnormal during palpitations. That is the key question a doctor will try to answer.

At night, the usual story is this. Something in your body or brain raises an alarm. Your nervous system releases stress hormones. Your heart speeds up and beats harder, and the jolt wakes you. You then notice the pounding in a dark, quiet room, which can make it feel far worse than it is.

Which everyday causes are most likely?

The MedlinePlus list of palpitation causes reads like a list of things that also disrupt sleep:

  • Anxiety, stress, and fear. Worry does not switch off at bedtime. Our guide to anxiety and insomnia covers how the two feed each other.
  • Caffeine and nicotine. Late coffee, energy drinks, or a cigarette before bed can linger for hours. Cutting back often reduces palpitations, per MedlinePlus.
  • Alcohol. MedlinePlus lists excess alcohol as a cause. The NHLBI adds that heavy or binge drinking raises the risk of atrial fibrillation, and even modest amounts can trigger it in some people.
  • Medicines. Decongestants such as pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine, diet pills, and some asthma, blood pressure, and heart drugs can cause palpitations.
  • Stimulant drugs such as cocaine or methamphetamine.
  • Fever.

Could it be a panic attack or a nightmare?

Yes. Both are common reasons to wake with a pounding heart.

Nocturnal panic attacks. These are panic attacks that start during sleep. A 2005 review in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that 44% to 71% of people with panic disorder report at least one. The authors describe nocturnal panic as a non-REM event. It is not the same as a nightmare, a sleep terror, or sleep apnea. You wake suddenly in fear, often with no dream to explain it. Cognitive behavioral therapy that targets fear of body sensations has evidence behind it. The same review notes that controlled drug studies for nocturnal panic are lacking.

Nightmares and trauma. A vivid nightmare can leave your heart racing as you wake. MedlinePlus lists nightmares among the core re-experiencing symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). If your nightmares link back to a frightening event, ptsd.md covers the condition and its treatment.

Can sleep apnea make your heart pound at night?

It can. In obstructive sleep apnea, the airway closes again and again during sleep. Each pause drops your oxygen, and your body fights to restart breathing. The NHLBI says this effort can damage your organs and blood vessels over time. It raises the risk of atrial fibrillation, hard-to-control high blood pressure, heart attack, heart failure, and stroke.

The NHLBI lists these signs of sleep apnea:

  • Loud, frequent snoring
  • Breathing that stops and starts
  • Gasping for air during sleep
  • Daytime sleepiness, morning headache, and dry mouth
  • Waking often to urinate
  • Insomnia, which is more common in women with the condition

You may not know you gasp or stop breathing. A bed partner often notices first. If this sounds like you, read our guide to the signs of sleep apnea.

What medical conditions can cause it?

  • Atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias. The NHLBI says AFib can cause palpitations, a sense that your heart is fluttering, pounding, or beating too hard or fast. The most common symptom is fatigue. Others are chest pain, dizziness, fainting, and trouble breathing when lying down. Untreated AFib can lead to stroke and heart failure.
  • Overactive thyroid. The NIDDK lists a rapid or irregular heartbeat, nervousness, trouble sleeping, sweating, and weight loss despite a bigger appetite. Hyperthyroidism also raises the risk of AFib.
  • Low blood sugar during sleep. This mainly affects people who take insulin or some other diabetes drugs. The NIDDK says blood glucose can drop overnight and stay low for hours. Signs include nightmares, crying out, sweat that soaks your sheets, and waking tired or confused. A fast or irregular heartbeat is a general sign of low blood sugar.
  • Menopause. The Menopause Society says hot flashes affect up to 80% of women and last a mean of 7 to 10 years. Some women feel a rapid heartbeat during a flash. Night sweats are hot flashes that happen in sleep. See our guide to menopause and insomnia.
  • Other causes. MedlinePlus adds heart valve disease, low potassium, and low blood oxygen.

When is a pounding heart an emergency?

MedlinePlus says to call 911 if palpitations come with any of these:

  • Chest pain
  • Shortness of breath
  • Loss of consciousness
  • Unusual sweating
  • Dizziness or lightheadedness

See a provider soon if you have never had palpitations before, if they are new or different, or if you often feel extra beats (more than 6 per minute, or in groups of 3 or more). The same applies if your resting pulse is over 100 without exercise, anxiety, or fever. Heart disease or risk factors such as high blood pressure, diabetes, or high cholesterol also warrant a visit.

What tests might a doctor order?

Your doctor will ask what the episodes feel like. Is the beat fast or slow, regular or irregular? Does it start and stop suddenly? Does it follow a nightmare, a drink, or a missed meal? MedlinePlus lists an ECG first, then other tests if a rhythm problem turns up. Common steps include:

  1. Electrocardiogram (ECG). A quick recording of your heart rhythm in the office.
  2. Holter monitor. A portable ECG worn for 24 hours, or another monitor worn for 2 weeks or longer. This matters for night symptoms, since a daytime ECG may look normal.
  3. Echocardiogram. An ultrasound of the heart's structure and valves.
  4. Blood tests. Your doctor may check thyroid levels, potassium, or blood sugar, since each can drive palpitations.
  5. Sleep study. If apnea is possible, the NHLBI says a sleep study shows which type you have and how severe it is.

Before the visit, keep a short log. Note the time, what you felt, your pulse if you can check it, and what you ate or drank that evening.

The bottom line

Waking with a pounding heart is usually your stress response doing its job at the wrong moment. Panic, nightmares, alcohol, caffeine, hot flashes, and low blood sugar are the common triggers. Sleep apnea and arrhythmias such as AFib are less common but important to rule out, because treatment protects your heart. Chest pain, fainting, or shortness of breath means call 911. Otherwise, see your doctor if it is new, frequent, or irregular.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal to wake up with a fast heartbeat once in a while?

An occasional episode after a bad dream, a late drink, or a stressful day is common and usually harmless. MedlinePlus says palpitations are not serious most of the time. It is worth seeing a doctor if it is new for you, happens often, or feels irregular.

How can I tell a nocturnal panic attack from a heart problem?

You often cannot tell on your own, which is why a first episode deserves a check. Intense fear with no dream to explain it fits nocturnal panic. Fainting, chest pain, or a beat that feels irregular needs a prompt medical check. An ECG or a wearable heart monitor can record what is really happening.

Can a glass of wine at night cause a racing heart?

It can for some people. MedlinePlus lists excess alcohol as a cause of palpitations. The NHLBI says binge drinking raises the risk of atrial fibrillation, and even modest amounts can trigger it in some people. If episodes follow drinking, try skipping alcohol and see if they stop.

What should I do in the moment when I wake up with my heart pounding?

Sit up, breathe slowly, and check your pulse if you can. Note whether the beat is fast and regular or irregular. If you have chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, or you pass out, call 911. If it settles, write down the time and details for your doctor.

Does a smartwatch help figure out nighttime palpitations?

Some watches can record a single-lead ECG and flag an irregular rhythm, which can give your doctor a useful clue. They do not replace a medical-grade Holter or event monitor. Bring any recordings to your appointment rather than relying on them for a diagnosis.
